01

Changes in this revision

  • Hosting. GitHub Pages is out — see section 02. Private repo, Cloudflare Pages.
  • Free roaming, staged districts. The whole current district is open to walk from the moment it unlocks; its ruins can be solved in any order; clearing all of them opens the next district. Movement is never gated on a query — a checkpoint is.
  • Seeded questions, not seeded data. Better than revision 2 in every direction: one cacheable dataset for all 3,000 players, no per-player storage, and sharing an answer still fails because your question names a different target.
  • Live verdicts. Section 08 is new: the whole submission path, its latency budget, and what it costs at three thousand people. A student learns whether they were right in about a tenth of a second, and every verdict says something they can act on.
  • A known fleet. Every student is on a MacBook Air or Pro, which retires the hardware risk entirely and lets the world budget go up rather than down.
  • This is a spec, not a plan. Section 13 is the work breakdown to implement against, with contracts between packages so parallel work joins cleanly, and acceptance tests that don't need me watching.
02

Hosting

No host hides client-side code. itch.io doesn’t either.

Whatever you deploy, the browser downloads it and devtools shows it — itch.io serves your bundle from html-classic.itch.zone and a student reads it in the Sources panel exactly as they would on Pages. What itch.io actually prevents is casual repository browsing: readable source, comments, commit history. That’s a real difference, but it’s a smaller one than it looks.

itch.io also carries a specific problem for us: HTML5 games run inside an iframe on a rotating subdomain, and Google refuses OAuth in embedded contexts. Ask #4 in section 13 stops working. Supabase allowed-origins become a moving target too.

Cloudflare Pages, deploying from a private GitHub repo.

Free, and it does what you actually wanted: the repo stays private, so nobody browses your source or your commits. The deployed bundle is Vite-minified with source maps off — machine-readable, not pleasant. Unlimited static bandwidth, which matters when 3,000 first-loads is roughly 15 GB. Custom domain, instant rollback if a level is broken at 09:00.

Fallbacks: Netlify (100 GB/month, enough) and Vercel (fine for a university course, though its free tier carries a non-commercial clause).

The real protection is that the client holds nothing worth stealing.

That is what revision 2 bought. Level text arrives one at a time, hints and solutions live in a schema the player role cannot see, XP is server state, and grading happens where the student has no keyboard. If somebody reads the whole bundle, they learn how the game is drawn and nothing about the answers. Design for that, and hosting stops being a security decision.

04

Roaming and the districts

How movement works

The city is one continuous space and you walk it on foot, WASD, third person. Nothing teleports you and no query moves you. What changes is what’s open: a district’s militia checkpoints — collapsed flyovers, welded shutters, a barricaded Metro mouth — hold until that district is cleared.

Inside the current district you go where you like and take the ruins in any order. They all teach the same topic band, so order within a district carries no pedagogical weight. Clear all of them and the checkpoints open.

Finding your way without a map

Unsolved ruins in the current district burn a sodium beacon visible through the dust; everything outside it is dark. The map key (M) draws only what you’ve walked past. There is no level list, no “7 of 20”, and no view of the final destination — you learn how far you’ve come by looking back.

Seeded questions, shared world

Every player downloads the same dataset. What differs is the question: the target is chosen by hashtext(player_id || ruin), so one student is asked for the stall in ward K-7 and their neighbour for B-2. Same tables, same query shape, different answer.

This is strictly better than seeding the data per player. The dataset caches once for all 3,000, the server stores nothing per player, and a leaked answer is still worthless. What does travel between students is the method — the shape of the SQL — and that’s them teaching each other, which is the point of the exercise.

Surveying pays

Walking within 20 m of any ruin in the current district marks it surveyed and pays +5 XP, once. Exploration is rewarded, sequence stays intact, and it gives the admin view a clean signal for who is exploring versus who is stuck.

Grade the plan, not the text

The piece doing the heavy lifting, and it costs almost nothing. Before comparing results the judge runs EXPLAIN (FORMAT JSON) and reads the node tree — which relations were actually scanned, whether a join happened, whether there was an aggregate or a subplan. Regexes over SQL text are trivially fooled; a plan is what Postgres truly intends to do.

-- vault.gate for ruin 20
require_relations : ['dilli.crossings', 'dilli.convoys', 'dilli.wards']
require_nodes     : ['Join', 'SubPlan']
forbid            : ['Nested Loop without join filter']   -- the cartesian trap

-- what a student sees when the rows are right but the method isn't
Correct rows — but the bridge needs all three registers joined.
Your plan touched only dilli.crossings.

It doubles as teaching: a cartesian product at ruin 20 gets named as one, out of the same EXPLAIN output your L13 and L14 lectures cover.

07

DQL only — enforced three ways

Nothing but SELECT and WITH … SELECT reaches the database, and each layer would hold alone:

  • Statement kind. The judge parses and rejects anything that isn’t a single query statement, before a connection opens.
  • Role grants. dilli_player holds SELECT on the dilli schema and nothing else, anywhere. No CREATE, no TEMP, no function execution.
  • Read-only transaction. Every execution is wrapped in BEGIN READ ONLY, so even a privilege mistake cannot write.

The practice engine enforces the same rule, so nobody learns a habit locally that the judge rejects.

So how do L1 and L2 still get taught?

By reading the evidence rather than issuing the command — which is what an analyst on a strange database does on day one anyway.

  • Constraints and keys from information_schema.table_constraints and key_column_usage. “Which column can’t repeat, and why was this row rejected?” is a SELECT.
  • DDL as an artefact: a column present in one manifest and not its older twin, read out of information_schema.columns.
  • DML and TCL in a txn_log table — which writes committed, which rolled back, so what the ledger should say now.
  • DCL in role_table_grants: who could still read the manifest after the city fell.
08

Submission and evaluation at scale

Two paths, and the split is what makes this affordable. Run is local and free — it absorbs the eight or nine queries out of ten that are a student poking at the data. Submit is the only path that scores, and it is the one that has to be fast, honest and cheap when three thousand people are doing it.

#StepWhereTypical
1Run — iterate freely, unlimitedPGlite, in the tab1–5 ms, no network
2Submit — POST /judge with JWT + {ruin, sql}client → edge30–40 ms RTT to Mumbai
3Verify the session JWTedge, project secret, no round trip<1 ms

Server work is 10–20 ms; the student sees a verdict in 60–120 ms. That is inside the window where a UI feels instant, so no optimistic guessing on the client — it would buy nothing and a wrong “correct” is trust you don’t get back.

The plan gate is also admission control

This is the part worth understanding, because it is what keeps a free instance standing. EXPLAIN reports estimated rows and cost before a single row is read. Fifty students writing a three-way cross join at ruin 20 is not a hypothetical — it is Thursday. So the judge refuses any plan whose estimate exceeds a million rows, in microseconds, without executing anything:

-- one gate, three jobs: method, anti-cheat, and load shedding
if plan.rows  > 1_000_000  or plan.cost > 50_000:
    reject("That query would scan the whole city. Look at what you're joining on —
            the plan says 4.1 billion rows before any filter.")

The 2-second statement_timeout is the backstop, not the defence. If runaway queries reach execution at all, fifty of them at once is fifty CPU-seconds per second on a shared instance — which is how you lose an event. Rejecting on the estimate costs nothing and teaches more, because the student is shown the number that condemned their query.

The numbers at 3,000

  • ~30 submissions per player over a 100-minute session, wrong answers included — call it 90,000 submissions total, averaging 15 per second. Design headroom: 100/s.
  • Postgres work per submission is two sub-millisecond queries against tables of a few hundred rows. Throughput is not the constraint; connections are.
  • ~105,000 Edge Function calls including briefs, hints and surveys, against 500,000 a month free.
  • ~210 MB of verdict JSON against 5 GB of free egress; a 36 MB attempts table with sql_text capped at 2,000 characters.

Three things that must be right or it falls over

  • Use the transaction-mode pooler, never a direct connection. Port 6543, not 5432. The free tier gives roughly 60 direct connections; Supavisor multiplexes thousands of clients onto a small backend pool. With postgres.js this also means prepare: false — transaction pooling cannot carry prepared statements between statements, and this is the single most likely way an implementation breaks production while everything works locally.
  • Static assets never touch Supabase. Three thousand people pulling a 5 MB bundle in the first minute is about 250 MB/s. Cloudflare absorbs that without noticing; a free Supabase project would burn its entire 5 GB egress in ten minutes and then serve errors.
  • Pre-warm the project. A free project pauses after a week idle and cold-starts in about 30 seconds — which the first hundred students would experience as a broken game. Hit it the evening before.

What the student sees

Never a bare “wrong”. Every verdict says something they can act on.

VerdictWhat appears
okFragment reveals, XP ticks up, the ruin’s beacon goes dark. If it was the district’s last ruin, the checkpoint opens.

XP and hints

-- opening balance
start              100
-- earn
ruin solved        +20   (20 ruins → +400)
ruin surveyed      +5    (once each, on foot)
-- spend
hint               -10
reveal solution    -30   (only after hints are spent)

-- hint allowance
ruins 1–10         1 hint each
ruins 11–20        2 hints each

-- clean run   100 + 400 + 100          = 600
-- typical     100 + 400 + 100 − 60 − 30 = 510

Rules the server enforces

  • Balance may go negative. A student stuck at 0 XP with a hint they can’t afford is a support ticket at 2 a.m. Debt is recorded, ranks last, blocks nothing.
  • Reveal appears only when that ruin’s hints are spent — checked server-side, not by hiding a button.
  • Hints and solutions are fetched after the deduction, one at a time. Buying a hint is a server event; nothing is pre-loaded into the client to be read early.
  • Survey XP is granted by the judge on a position claim the server sanity-checks against walking speed, so you can’t claim twenty surveys in one second.
10

The terminal

  • Docked right, floatable. 420 px right dock by default; drag the title bar to undock, drag the left edge to resize, ` collapses. Position persists per player.
  • Two buttons, not one. Ctrl+Enter runs locally — instant, free, unlimited. Submit sends it to the judge. Students learn the difference between trying something and claiming it, which is worth teaching on its own.
  • Cleared at every ruin. New ruin, new blank terminal. The previous ruin’s queries stay in a scrollback you can read but not copy from.
  • No paste. paste, drop and middle-click cancelled on the editor; clue and schema panels user-select:none. Devtools defeats it — but with seeded questions there is nothing worth pasting.
  • Real Postgres errors, caret and all. ERROR: column "spice" does not exist is the artefact they need to learn to read.
  • The verdict is live. Submit shows a checking state for a minimum of 150 ms so it does not flash, then the judge's answer — typically inside 120 ms. Section 08 lists every verdict and its wording.
  • No timer. Elapsed time is recorded for you and never shown to them.
  • Only opens inside a ruin’s radius, and only for a ruin in the unlocked district. Elsewhere the key does nothing and the HUD says why.

Data model

-- ── who may play ──────────────────────────────────────────────
create table players (
  id            uuid primary key default gen_random_uuid(),
  email         text unique not null check (
                  email ~* '^[a-z0-9._%+-]+@(students\.)?example\.edu$'),
  university_id text not null,
  seed          int  not null,          -- picks this player's targets
  xp            int  not null default 100,
  district      int  not null default 1,
  solved        int[] not null default '{}',
  surveyed      int[] not null default '{}',
  started_at    timestamptz default now(),
  last_seen_at  timestamptz default now()
);

-- ── the trail ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
create table attempts (
  id bigserial primary key, player_id uuid references players,
  ruin int, sql_text text, verdict text,   -- ok | wrong_rows | wrong_method | error | locked
  plan_relations text[], ms int, pg_error text, at timestamptz default now()
);
create table spends (
  id bigserial primary key, player_id uuid references players,
  ruin int, kind text check (kind in ('hint','reveal')),
  cost int, at timestamptz default now()
);
create table journey (
  id bigserial primary key, player_id uuid references players,
  ruin int, surveyed_at timestamptz, first_attempt_at timestamptz,
  solved_at timestamptz, hints_used int default 0, attempts int default 0
);

-- ── the wall ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
create schema dilli;   -- the world.  dilli_player: SELECT
create schema vault;   -- targets, gates, hints, solutions.  dilli_player: nothing

revoke all on schema vault from dilli_player, anon, authenticated;
grant  usage on schema dilli to dilli_player;
grant  select on all tables in schema dilli to dilli_player;
alter role dilli_player set statement_timeout = '2s';
alter role dilli_player set default_transaction_read_only = on;

Contracts between packages

These are frozen before work starts, so world and server can be built in parallel and join without a merge argument at 3 a.m.

// A · judge — the only scoring path
POST /functions/v1/judge      // Authorization: Bearer <supabase jwt>
  → { ruin: int, sql: string }
  ← { verdict: 'ok'|'wrong_rows'|'wrong_method'|'error'|'locked'|'rate_limited',
      message: string,            // player-facing, never leaks the target
      rows?: object[],            // capped at 50
      xp: int, district: int, solved: int[],
      fragment?: string,          // on ok
      district_cleared?: boolean }

// B · level brief — one at a time, current district only
GET  /functions/v1/brief?ruin=7
  ← { ruin, district, name, brief: string,
      tables: [{ schema, table, columns: string[] }],
      hints_total: int, hints_bought: int, reveal_unlocked: boolean }

// C · paid reveals
POST /functions/v1/hint    → { ruin } ← { text, xp }
POST /functions/v1/reveal  → { ruin } ← { sql, xp }

// D · survey claim, speed-checked server-side
POST /functions/v1/survey  → { ruin, pos:[x,z] } ← { xp, surveyed: int[] }

// E · world data — static, cached in IndexedDB, identical for everyone
GET  /world/schema.sql        // CREATE TABLE ... for the dilli schema
GET  /world/rows.jsonl        // the shared dataset
GET  /world/ruins.json        // [{ n, district, name, pos:[x,z], archetype, radius }]
GET  /world/checkpoints.json  // [{ district, colliders:[[x,z,w,d]] }]

Risks

  • Free-tier Postgres at peak. The load-bearing risk. Graded submissions only — around 12 authoritative queries per second on small indexed tables — should sit fine on a shared instance, but it is untested at 3,000. Keep $25 of Supabase Pro available for the day, or split the cohort across two free projects by email hash.
  • A continuous open city is the biggest new cost in this revision. Fog capping draw distance at 150 m is what makes it affordable; without that, WP-3 overruns and takes WP-8’s time with it. If WP-3 isn’t holding 60 fps by 23:30, take the arena cut early rather than late.
  • Over-strict plan gates rejecting correct students is the failure I expect most, and it’s the worst one — it makes the game feel broken and unfair at the same time. Every gate needs at least three accepted phrasings tested in WP-8.
  • The fleet risk is gone, and it buys something. Every student on an M-series MacBook means WASM Postgres and the 3D world both have comfortable headroom — no capability gate, no low-spec fallback path, and WP-3 can afford a longer fog distance and denser rubble than revision 3 assumed. The remaining browser question is Safari, which is the macOS default: it runs PGlite fine, but it evicts site data after seven days of no interaction, so treat the cached world as a one-session convenience rather than storage. Test both browsers; recommend Chrome in the joining instructions, support Safari.
  • Retina. Cap devicePixelRatio at 1.5. On a 14-inch Pro the uncapped path renders roughly four times the pixels for no visible gain and turns a 60 fps scene into 30.
  • Untested at 3,000. One real section as a pilot before the main run is the only honest proof. If you can get one, take it.
