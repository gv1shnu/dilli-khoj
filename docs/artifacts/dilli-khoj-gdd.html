01

High concept

Dilli Khoj — "the search for Delhi" — is a single-player, browser-based 3D exploration game that teaches the practical SQL curriculum of a DBMS course.

The player walks the ruins of an imagined, long-abandoned Shahjahanabad and wakes its sealed archives one at a time. Each archive is a real SQL question graded against hidden data. Waking an archive restores light to that part of the city and opens the gate to the next.

The fantasy is quiet restoration — not competition or combat. You bring a dead city's records back to life by asking the right questions of them. Learning SQL is the literal mechanic by which the world heals.

02

Design pillars

  1. The curriculum is the map. The twenty questions follow Modules 1–8 in a fixed order, and that order is literally laid out as a route through the city. Progressing through the world is progressing through the syllabus.
  2. Experiment freely, commit deliberately. Practising a query is unlimited and free — local Postgres in the browser. Only the authoritative Submit touches the hidden grader. Curiosity is never taxed.
  3. Debt rations shortcuts, never learning. Hints and reveals cost XP and are gated by balance. Solving is always free and always available — a player can never be blocked from finishing, only from skipping.
  4. No dead ends, no failure states. A wrong submission costs nothing. No timer, no health, no losing. The only way forward is understanding.
  5. Plain language over jargon. Questions and hints describe the data outcome in everyday words and name the exact columns to use. Technique is suggested, never required — any safe query returning the right rows passes.
03

Audience & learning goals

3,000students, modern Mac browsers
₹0operating-cost target (free tier)
20ruins across 7 districts

The goal is fluent read-only SQL: SELECT and WITH … SELECT covering filtering, pattern matching, ordering and paging, string / numeric / date / null functions, aggregation and group-filtering, CASE, window functions, subqueries, and joins with a set operation.

Scope guard: the game is entirely read-only. Even the topics that are about writes (DDL / DML / TCL / DCL in Modules 1–2) are taught through evidence tables — catalog, audit, change-log and grant tables — so every concept is exercised with SELECT alone. No writes or privilege changes exist anywhere in the game.

04

World & narrative

Long after the noise faded, old Delhi went still. Its records — who lived where, what moved through the bazaars, how the wards were run — were sealed in scattered archives and slowly overgrown. The player walks the ruins of Shahjahanabad to wake those archives, one question at a time.

The onboarding never mentions "winning" or "finishing." There is no antagonist and no ending cutscene — the endgame is simply that every region has been brought back to light.

The twenty ruins map onto real Delhi landmarks, grouped into seven districts that unlock in sequence, each holding one band of the curriculum.

05

Core gameplay loop

  1. Walk the city (WASD / arrows, drag to look, Shift to run). Floating arrows trace a walkable route to your next archive.
  2. Arrive at a ruin — a broken place with a glowing amber (the sealed archive) rising from it. Press E to open it.
  3. Read what it asks for: a plain-language description, the exact rows and columns wanted, and a sample output. A separate schema browser shows the tables.
  4. Run queries freely against a local copy of the records — unlimited, free, offline-capable. Experiment as much as you like.
  5. Submit when confident. The archive checks your query for real against hidden records you can't see. A wrong answer costs nothing.
  6. Restore on success: the ruin wakes, its lights return, the region appears on your map, and the gate to the next area opens.
  7. Travel via the map to any restored place. Future areas stay sealed until the earlier archives are restored.

Optional at any archive: open a hint (small XP cost) or, once all hints are opened, reveal the full solution (larger cost). Both are opt-in shortcuts — never required.

06

World traversal

The world is a physical open world, not a menu of levels. Its layout encodes the curriculum so that walking the intended path visits the ruins in syllabus order.

  • Serpentine grid. The twenty ruins sit on a 5×4 grid stitched in snake order, so ruin N+1 is always physically adjacent to N. The route through town is the route through the course.
  • Toroidal wrap. Ground tiles seamlessly (720-unit tiles), so the city reads as boundless — walking off one edge continues the world rather than hitting a wall.
  • Physical gating. Locked regions are sealed by gates that will not admit the player until the prior archive is restored — enforced at the geometry level (canEnter), not just in the UI.
  • Guided routing. A breadth-first search over walkable cells lays down arrows that always point along a legal path to the current frontier, so a lost player is never truly lost.
  • Per-level kits. Each location is hand-authored with its own props and landmark silhouette — a stepwell descent, a cable bridge, a ridge ascent, market lanes.
  • Two maps. Players get an animated atlas where only restored regions are revealed and clickable; admins get a full, inspection-only atlas that never bypasses scoring.
07

The archives: question design

Every archive presents four things in order — title, description, sample output, and hints (opened on demand). The schema browser is a separate panel, not part of the prompt.

  • Descriptions state the required outcome in plain language and name the exact columns and ordering — 12–35 words. They recommend a technique where useful but never require it.
  • Sample output shows fictional, clearly-fake rows (e.g. sample_col_a) that can never be mistaken for the real answer.
  • Hints are short, concrete and jargon-free. Ruins 1–10 have one; 11–20 have two. The full solution unlocks only after every hint is opened.
  • Acceptance is by result, not by form. Each question carries a canonical solution plus structurally different accepted variants (LEFT JOIN vs UNION/NOT EXISTS, HAVING vs a subquery filter). Any safe query returning the correct set passes.

Reading Modules 1–2 with SELECT only

Schema, keys, constraints and the SQL command families normally imply writes and grants. Dilli Khoj teaches them through evidence tables the player only reads — catalog_columns, column_keys, foreign_keys, change_log (tagged DDL vs DML) and access_grants. That is why these questions name real columns like entity and attribute: those are the actual identifiers the player sees and types, not abstract theory terms.

08

Progression & unlocks

  • Strictly sequential. A ruin unlocks only when the previous one is cleared. The frontier is the first ruin not yet cleared.
  • Cleared ruins stay open for revisiting as non-scoring practice.
  • Server-authoritative in production. Solved state, XP, hint/reveal usage, survey state, unlocks and completion time are owned by the server. Offline play keeps the same shape but is a non-scoring practice proxy.

  • Help is a purchase gated by balance. You cannot open a hint or reveal you can't afford. A player taking maximum help every level runs out around level 13 and must solve the rest unaided — there is no buying your way to the finish.
  • Solving is always free and always available. Debt never blocks learning; only shortcuts are rationed.
  • A reveal solves nothing automatically. Even after revealing, the learner must submit a passing query. There is no path to the end without solving.
10

Grading & integrity

Two distinct execution surfaces keep practice cheap and grading trustworthy.

Run · practice

Runs in PGlite — Postgres compiled to WebAssembly, in a browser Web Worker — against the visible dataset. Free, unlimited, offline, never scored.

Submit · authoritative

Sent to a Supabase Edge Function judge that parses with libpg-query, enforces a read-only table allow-list and a timeout, runs against hidden data, and compares the result set to precomputed rows.

  • Three fixtures per question — one visible for practice, two hidden for grading — so memorising the visible answer does not pass.
  • Expected rows are computed, never hand-written — the canonical solution is run against each fixture by real Postgres during content generation.
  • Result-based acceptance with near-miss tests: known-wrong queries must fail; every accepted variant must pass.
  • Least privilege: the judge executes as a role that can only SELECT from fixture schemas; private answer and help tables are unreadable by players and by the executor role.
11

Revisits, leaderboard & endgame

Any cleared ruin reopens from the map as a revisit — a non-scoring practice attempt. Variants are deterministic from (player, ruin, visit count): the same tables with a different target or threshold, so a topic can be drilled without ever touching XP, unlocks or the leaderboard.

Leaderboard

  • Lists only players who have completed all twenty ruins.
  • Ranked by XP remaining (descending), then completion time (ascending, from server-recorded sign-up to first completion).
  • Shows name and XP — never email addresses — top twenty plus the current player's own position.

Endgame

Restoring the twentieth archive — Signature Bridge — lights the whole city. There is no forced ending screen; the open world remains to wander and revisit.

12

Interface, audio & art

A reopenable onboarding overlay explains movement, ruins & ambers, and the Run/Submit loop — deliberately saying nothing about "finishing." Controls: WASD/arrows to walk, drag to look, Shift to run, E to open an archive, M to mute ambience. Dialogs trap and restore focus; motion can be paused and honors reduced-motion preferences.

Regional soundscapes give each district its own generated ambience — river wind at the water gates, insects and birds on the wooded ridge, water drops in the stepwell, market murmur in the bazaars — built procedurally with the Web Audio API, so there are no large audio downloads. Art direction is low-poly, quiet and overgrown, with hand-placed landmark silhouettes per ruin; inspiration is credited to Exceletia by edusatyaki.

13

Technical architecture & content pipeline

  • Client: Vite + React 19 + TypeScript, 3D via Three.js; hosted as static assets on Cloudflare Workers.
  • Local practice DB: PGlite (Postgres in WebAssembly) in a Web Worker.
  • Backend: Supabase — Google auth (approved-domain allow-list), Postgres for authoritative state, and an Edge Function judge. State and grading are exposed only through RPCs; answers, help and fixtures are server-private.
  • CI/CD: GitHub Actions; main auto-deploys. Timezone pinned to Asia/Kolkata across content and test scripts for deterministic timestamps.

src/questions/catalog.ts is the single authoring source of truth. Generators derive everything from it: the client bundle (hint text and answers stripped — only a hintCount ships), the judge fixtures migration (hidden schemas and Postgres-computed expected rows), and a forward-only migration that updates player-facing copy in the live database. A reproducibility test asserts the committed fixtures are byte-identical to what the catalog generates, so authoring, the client and the grader can never silently drift apart.

Non-goals & open items

  • No writes. No INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE/DDL/DCL is ever run by a player; write-family topics are taught read-only by design.
  • No multiplayer, combat, or timers. The only competitive surface is the completers-only leaderboard.
  • Not yet deployed. This branch implements all twenty server-graded questions and trusted progression; the current hosted build still reports an older Ruin-06-only judge. Shipping requires pushing the new migrations and redeploying the judge.
  • Blind human review of each drafted question remains a production step before a classroom-wide release.
